Healthy plant care is less about rigid calendars and more about noticing light, moisture, growth and change. LeafNote is a practical field guide for everyday indoor plant decisions.

01 / PLANT CHECK

Start with what you can observe.

Select a broad symptom and soil condition. The tool suggests questions and conservative next checks rather than pretending to diagnose a plant from two inputs.

OBSERVATION PATH

    Many symptoms have multiple causes. Check the plant species, care label, recent changes, roots/potting conditions and pests before making a major intervention.
    02 / WATERING

    Water the plant you have, not the calendar.

    Check before you pour.

    Water needs change with plant type, pot size, potting mix, season, temperature, humidity and light. A fixed “every seven days” rule can be too frequent in one room and too infrequent in another.

    Useful habit: learn how your plant's potting mix changes between freshly watered and ready-to-check-again conditions. Species-specific guidance still matters.
    03 / LIGHT

    “Bright” is not a location.

    Watch the path of light.

    Window direction, distance from glass, outdoor obstructions, curtains and season all change the light a plant receives. “Bright indirect light” generally describes useful illumination without prolonged harsh direct sun, but species tolerances differ.

    Observe: whether growth leans strongly, becomes unusually stretched, scorches, or changes after moving the plant.
    04 / POTTING

    Roots need a workable environment.

    Repot for a reason.

    A larger pot is not automatically better. Repotting may be useful when roots are genuinely crowded, the mix has broken down, drainage is unsuitable, or plant-specific care calls for it. Oversized containers can hold moisture differently from the previous setup.

    Before repotting: identify the species, inspect root condition when appropriate, and choose a potting medium and container suited to that plant.
    05 / LEAF SIGNALS

    A symptom is a clue, not a verdict.

    The same visible change can have several explanations. Use patterns and recent history rather than diagnosing from a single leaf.

    Yellow leaves

    Can occur with normal aging, moisture problems, root stress, light changes and other causes. Look at which leaves are affected and what changed recently.

    Brown tips

    May relate to moisture consistency, salts, humidity, root conditions or species-specific sensitivity. Avoid assuming one universal cause.

    Drooping

    Can appear when a plant is too dry, too wet, heat-stressed, root-stressed or adjusting. Check conditions before watering automatically.

    Stretching

    Longer spacing or leaning can be associated with insufficient usable light for some plants. Compare new growth with older growth.

    06 / HOME SAFETY

    Plants share the room with people and pets.

    Pet & child safety

    Some houseplants can be irritating or toxic if chewed or swallowed. Identify plants accurately and use authoritative poison-safety or veterinary resources for exposure concerns.

    Pest products

    Follow the label exactly for any pesticide or treatment product, including ventilation, protective equipment, application and storage directions.

    Mold & decay

    Persistent wetness, odor or decaying material deserves attention. Correct the moisture condition and use appropriate guidance rather than masking the symptom.

    07 / FAQ

    Houseplant questions in plain language.

    How often should I water my houseplants?

    There is no universal interval. Plant type, pot, potting mix, light, temperature and season affect water use. Check the plant and its growing medium, then follow species-appropriate guidance rather than relying only on a calendar.

    Why are my plant's leaves turning yellow?

    Yellowing can have many causes, including natural leaf aging, watering conditions, root stress, light changes and pests. Look at the pattern, recent care changes and the plant's specific requirements before acting.

    Should every plant pot have a drainage hole?

    Many commonly grown houseplants are easier to manage in containers that allow excess water to drain, but container systems and plant needs vary. Understand how water moves through the specific setup you are using.

    Does misting increase humidity enough for tropical plants?

    Misting may temporarily wet leaf surfaces but does not necessarily create a stable room-level humidity change. If humidity matters for a species, measure conditions and use an appropriate broader strategy.

    When should I repot a houseplant?

    Consider the plant's root space, potting mix condition, drainage and species needs. Repotting simply because time has passed is less useful than having a clear horticultural reason.

    Can I identify a plant problem from one photo?

    A photo can provide clues, but diagnosis often benefits from information about roots, soil moisture, light, pests, watering history and recent changes. Treat visual symptoms as evidence to investigate, not automatic proof of one cause.
